## Part 1 — SDLC Fundamentals and Models (25 questions)

### SDLC basics and phases

#### Q1. What is the software development life cycle?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The software development life cycle is the structured set of phases a product passes through, from requirements to retirement. QA joins at requirements, not only at test execution, because defects found earlier cost less to fix.

</details>

#### Q2. What are the typical phases of an SDLC, and what happens in each?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Most life cycles include planning and feasibility, requirements analysis, design, implementation, testing, deployment, and maintenance. Each phase produces artifacts the next phase consumes: requirements become designs, designs become code, and code becomes a tested increment. QA contributes reviews and test artifacts from requirements onward, not only at the testing phase.

</details>

#### Q3. Where does testing fit in the SDLC, and why is "testing phase" a misleading term?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The testing phase is when most test execution happens, but quality activities span the whole life cycle. QA reviews requirements, designs test cases, prepares data and environments, and plans regression long before execution. Treating testing as a single late phase is what creates release surprises.

</details>

#### Q4. What is the difference between the SDLC and the STLC?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The SDLC is the end-to-end process for building and maintaining a product; the STLC is the testing workstream inside it. The STLC runs requirement analysis, test planning, test design, environment setup, execution, and closure, often in parallel with development phases. In release QA, STLC outputs are the evidence used for go/no-go.

</details>

#### Q5. Why does involving QA during requirements analysis reduce release risk?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Early QA review turns vague requirements into testable acceptance criteria and exposes contradictions before code exists. It also lets QA size risk, plan regression, and identify missing non-functional requirements early. Fixing a requirement defect in analysis is far cheaper than discovering it during release testing.

</details>

### Waterfall

#### Q6. What characterizes the Waterfall model?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Waterfall is a sequential model: each phase completes and is approved before the next begins, with heavy documentation and formal change control. Testing usually follows implementation, so feedback arrives late. It suits stable, well-understood scope and environments where documentation and auditability matter.

</details>

#### Q7. What are the main advantages and disadvantages of Waterfall for a release QA team?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Waterfall gives clear milestones, complete documentation, and a predictable audit trail. The cost is late feedback: requirement and design defects surface during test execution, changes are expensive, and the test window is often compressed. Release QA responds by front-loading requirement and design reviews and protecting enough regression time.

</details>

#### Q8. When is Waterfall an appropriate choice?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Choose Waterfall when requirements are stable, scope is fixed by contract or regulation, and change is expensive or slow, such as some hardware or compliance releases. Even then, add early reviews, test planning, and entry criteria so the late test phase is not the first quality checkpoint.

</details>

### V-model

#### Q9. What is the V-model, and how does it relate development and test levels?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The V-model pairs each development level on the left with a verification level on the right: requirements with acceptance testing, system design with system testing, architecture with integration testing, and module design with unit testing. Test planning begins on the left, so each level knows how it will be verified. It makes coverage and entry and exit expectations explicit.

</details>

#### Q10. How do entry and exit criteria work at each V-model level?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Entry criteria confirm the level can start: approved artifacts, a ready environment, and test data. Exit criteria confirm it can finish: planned tests executed, defects closed or accepted, and coverage and pass rates met. These gates make release readiness evidence-based instead of a matter of opinion.

</details>

#### Q11. What are the limitations of the V-model in practice?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The V-model is still sequential, so late requirement changes cascade through designs and tests. It can become document-heavy, and it assumes requirements can be baselined early. Mitigate with reviews, early test design, and incremental delivery where the domain allows it.

</details>

### Iterative, incremental, and spiral

#### Q12. What is the difference between iterative and incremental development?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Iterative development repeats cycles to refine the product, while incremental development delivers it in functional slices. They are often combined: each increment is built through several iterations. For QA, every increment needs its own tests plus cumulative regression and integration checks.

</details>

#### Q13. What is the spiral model, and what makes it different?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The spiral model is risk-driven and iterative: each loop sets an objective, analyzes risk, builds and tests an increment, then plans the next loop. Prototypes and risk mitigation happen early, which suits large, uncertain, or safety-critical work. It needs disciplined risk expertise and can be costly for small projects.

</details>

#### Q14. How does QA strategy change in an iterative or incremental lifecycle?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

QA tests each increment as it is built and keeps a regression suite that grows with the product. Cross-increment integration, performance, and end-to-end flows need explicit planning because no single iteration owns them. Risk is reassessed every cycle, and release readiness is judged on the cumulative product, not the last increment alone.

</details>

#### Q15. When would you choose an iterative or incremental approach over Waterfall?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Choose iterative or incremental when requirements are uncertain, early feedback matters, or the product must deliver value before everything is finished. Waterfall fits better when scope is fixed and change is genuinely expensive. The decision is about feedback cost and risk, not fashion.

</details>

### Agile and Scrum

#### Q16. What does "Agile" mean in practice, beyond the ceremonies?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Agile is a set of values and principles: short feedback loops, collaboration with the customer, working software, and responding to change. Scrum is one framework that implements those ideas with roles, ceremonies, and artifacts. Agile does not remove testing; it moves quality work into every iteration.

</details>

#### Q17. What are the three Scrum roles, and how does QA interact with each?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The Product Owner prioritizes the backlog and accepts completed work; the Scrum Master facilitates the process and removes impediments; the Developers include QA and everyone who builds the increment. QA works with the Product Owner on acceptance criteria, with developers on testability and automation, and with the Scrum Master when environments or dependencies block progress.

</details>

#### Q18. What are the Scrum ceremonies, and what is QA's contribution to each?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

In sprint planning, QA helps size testing work and clarify acceptance criteria. In the daily Scrum, QA surfaces blockers and test progress; in the sprint review, QA demonstrates verified behavior and discusses quality; in the retrospective, QA raises process improvements. Backlog refinement is where much of the test-design thinking happens.

</details>

#### Q19. What are the Scrum artifacts, and how does QA use them?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The product backlog holds prioritized stories and acceptance criteria; the sprint backlog holds the tasks for the current sprint, including test tasks; the increment is the potentially shippable result. QA turns acceptance criteria into tests, tracks test work in the sprint backlog, and verifies the increment against the definition of done.

</details>

#### Q20. What is the definition of done, and why does it matter for release quality?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The definition of done is the team's shared checklist for a completed item: code reviewed, tests written and passing, acceptance criteria met, documentation updated, and no open critical defects. It stops "done" from meaning different things to different people. A stable definition of done makes release quality predictable.

</details>

#### Q21. How does release planning work in Scrum when the team delivers every sprint?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Sprint delivery does not mean every sprint is released; release planning selects which increments form a release and sets the release goal, date, dependencies, and risk. QA assesses cumulative regression, non-functional readiness, and unresolved defects. The Product Owner decides scope, while release QA provides the evidence for go/no-go.

</details>

### Shift-left, shift-right, DevOps, and release vs deployment

#### Q22. What is shift-left testing, and what does it look like in practice?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Shift-left moves quality activities earlier: reviewing requirements, designing tests before code, writing unit and component tests, running static analysis, and gating changes in continuous integration. It catches defects while they are cheap and gives developers fast feedback. It complements, rather than replaces, system and release-level testing.

</details>

#### Q23. What is shift-right testing, and when is it appropriate?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Shift-right tests in production or after release: canary releases, feature flags, synthetic monitoring, chaos experiments, and A/B tests. It validates real traffic, configuration, and operational behavior that pre-release environments cannot fully reproduce. It requires observability, rollback, and a controlled blast radius.

</details>

#### Q24. How does DevOps change the QA role?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

DevOps makes delivery a shared, automated pipeline, so QA moves from late manual gatekeeping to building quality into continuous integration and delivery. That means automated regression, quality gates, environments as code, and monitoring production signals. Release QA still owns release criteria and risk decisions, but backs them with pipeline evidence.

</details>

#### Q25. What is the difference between a release and a deployment?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Deployment moves artifacts into an environment; release makes the functionality available to users. Feature flags and dark launches can separate the two, so code can be deployed without being released. Release QA cares about deployment health and user-visible readiness, which are related but not the same decision.

</details>

## Part 2 — Test Levels and Testing Types (40 questions)

### Test levels

#### Q26. What is a test level?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A test level is a stage of testing defined by its scope and the artifacts it verifies: unit or component, integration, system, and acceptance. Each level has different goals, environments, and entry and exit criteria.

</details>

#### Q27. What is unit testing, and who owns it?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Unit testing exercises the smallest testable pieces of code, such as functions, methods, or components, in isolation. It is usually owned by developers and uses white-box knowledge of the implementation. It is fast and precise, but it does not prove that integrated features work for users.

</details>

#### Q28. What is integration testing, and what are common integration strategies?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Integration testing verifies that components exchange data and behave correctly together. Common strategies are big bang, where everything is combined at once; top-down, which uses stubs for lower layers; bottom-up, which uses drivers for higher layers; and sandwich or hybrid, which combines both directions. The choice depends on architecture, risk, and which components are available first.

</details>

#### Q29. Compare big bang and incremental integration testing.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Big bang integration combines all components at once, so it starts quickly but makes failures hard to isolate. Incremental integration adds components in a planned order, which exposes interface defects earlier but requires stubs or drivers and more planning. For complex systems, incremental integration is usually the lower-risk choice.

</details>

#### Q30. What is system testing, and what does it cover?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

System testing exercises the complete, integrated product against its requirements in a production-like environment. It covers functional workflows and relevant non-functional behavior such as performance, security, and usability. It is the last major pre-release level before acceptance and the main source of release regression evidence.

</details>

#### Q31. What is acceptance testing, and how does UAT fit?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Acceptance testing judges whether the product meets business and user needs well enough to be accepted for release. User acceptance testing, or UAT, is a common form in which real users validate end-to-end business workflows. Acceptance is driven by agreed criteria and sign-off, not by how many internal test cases passed.

</details>

#### Q32. What is the difference between alpha and beta testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Alpha testing is internal and controlled, often performed at the developer's site before external release. Beta testing gives a limited release to real users in real environments and collects feedback before general availability. Beta testing finds environment and usage problems that a controlled lab misses.

</details>

#### Q33. What is the difference between a test level and a test type?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A test level describes where in the lifecycle testing happens and what scope it covers, such as unit, integration, system, or acceptance. A test type describes the aspect being tested, such as functional, performance, security, or usability. The same type can run at several levels, for example performance testing at system level and again as part of acceptance.

</details>

### Functional and non-functional testing

#### Q34. What is functional testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Functional testing verifies what the system does against requirements and business rules: inputs, outputs, workflows, calculations, and error handling. It is usually black-box and scenario-based, and it includes regression and acceptance checks. It answers whether the product does the right thing.

</details>

#### Q35. What is non-functional testing, and why does release QA care?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Non-functional testing verifies how well the system behaves: performance, security, usability, reliability, and compatibility. These qualities are often invisible in a feature demo but decisive in production. Release QA needs evidence against thresholds, because missing non-functional requirements are a common source of release risk.

</details>

### Smoke testing

#### Q36. What is smoke testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Smoke testing is a shallow, broad check that a build is stable enough for deeper testing. It covers critical paths such as sign-in, the main workflow, and a few key integrations, and it is usually automated and run on every new build. If smoke fails, the build is rejected early instead of wasting a full regression cycle.

</details>

#### Q37. When does smoke testing run, and what happens if it fails?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Smoke testing runs on every new build, at the start of a CI pipeline, and again before a build is promoted to a test environment. If it fails, the build is returned to development and deeper testing is blocked. Repeated smoke failures are also a process signal that build quality or pipeline checks need attention.

</details>

#### Q38. What is the difference between smoke testing and build verification testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The terms are often used interchangeably, but build verification testing is the broader build-acceptance check: the build is complete, installable or deployable, correctly configured, and its core functions work. Smoke testing is usually the fast critical-path subset. Define the terms in your test strategy so the team knows which gate a build has passed.

</details>

### Sanity testing

#### Q39. What is sanity testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Sanity testing is a narrow check after a small change or fix, confirming that the affected area works and that fuller testing is worth running. It is targeted rather than broad, and it often uses only a few high-value cases. It answers whether this change is stable enough to continue.

</details>

#### Q40. What is the difference between smoke and sanity testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Smoke testing is broad and shallow across critical paths, and it decides whether a new build is accepted for testing. Sanity testing is narrow and focused on a specific change, and it decides whether that change is ready for broader tests. They can overlap in small projects, but they answer different gate questions.

</details>

### Regression testing

#### Q41. What is regression testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Regression testing re-runs existing tests to confirm that a change has not broken behavior that used to work. It is not the same as verifying the fix itself; it protects the surrounding product. Because the suite grows with the product, selection and automation are essential.

</details>

#### Q42. When should regression testing run?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Regression testing runs after code changes, fixes, merges, dependency updates, and configuration or environment changes. In continuous integration, a fast subset runs on every change and a fuller suite runs before release. Release QA sets the cadence and scope according to risk.

</details>

#### Q43. How do you select regression tests when time is limited?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Use risk-based selection: the areas touched by the change, their integrations, high-traffic or high-revenue paths, recent defect clusters, and critical non-functional checks. Impact analysis, code changes, and historical failures help narrow the set, while the stable core should be automated. Record what was skipped and the residual risk for the release decision.

</details>

#### Q44. What is the difference between regression testing and retesting?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Retesting, also called confirmation testing, checks that a specific defect is fixed by re-running the failing scenario. Regression testing checks that the fix and other changes did not break existing behavior elsewhere. Retesting is narrow and defect-driven; regression is broad and change-driven.

</details>

### Retesting and confirmation

#### Q45. What is retesting, and what should a tester verify beyond the original failure?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Retesting re-executes the scenario that failed after a fix and confirms the expected result. Beyond the original case, check related boundary and negative cases and nearby workflows for side effects, then run targeted regression. Closing a defect because one path now passes is a common and expensive mistake.

</details>

### Build verification

#### Q46. What is build verification testing, and how does it differ from regression?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Build verification testing confirms that a build is complete, deployable, correctly configured, and that its core functions work before deeper testing starts. It is a gate on the build itself and is usually fast and broad. Regression testing is larger and verifies existing behavior after changes, so BVT can be the first part of a regression cycle but not the whole of it.

</details>

### Performance testing family

#### Q47. What is performance testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Performance testing evaluates speed, responsiveness, stability, and resource use under a defined workload. It answers whether the system meets performance requirements under realistic conditions. Load, stress, spike, soak, volume, and scalability testing are all ways of exploring that question.

</details>

#### Q48. What is load testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Load testing exercises the system at expected and peak workload to verify response times, throughput, and error rates against targets. It uses a realistic mix of users, transactions, and data. Load testing answers whether the system can handle the traffic the business expects.

</details>

#### Q49. What is stress testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Stress testing pushes the system beyond expected capacity until it degrades or fails, to find its breaking point and observe how it fails. The goal is to learn whether it recovers gracefully, sheds load, or loses data. Stress testing is not the same as load testing at normal peaks.

</details>

#### Q50. What is spike testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Spike testing applies a sudden, sharp increase in load, often followed by a sharp decrease, to test elasticity, queuing, autoscaling, and recovery. It simulates viral traffic, batch bursts, or a sudden retry storm. The focus is the transition and recovery, not sustained capacity.

</details>

#### Q51. What is soak or endurance testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Soak testing runs a sustained normal-to-peak load for hours or days to expose problems that appear over time, such as memory leaks, resource exhaustion, log growth, and gradual degradation. It answers whether the system remains stable for the length of a real operating period. Short load tests cannot reveal these failures.

</details>

#### Q52. What is volume testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Volume testing stresses the system with large amounts of data, such as a big database, large files, or deep queues, rather than high user concurrency. It checks data processing, queries, storage, and retention limits. Volume and load are different axes and usually need separate tests.

</details>

#### Q53. What is scalability testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Scalability testing checks whether the system can handle growth by adding resources, such as more CPU, nodes, or database capacity, while maintaining performance. It identifies bottlenecks and the cost of scaling. It overlaps with load and stress testing but focuses on the growth path and efficiency.

</details>

#### Q54. What is the difference between response time and throughput?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Response time is how long one request or transaction takes, often reported as a percentile such as p95 or p99. Throughput is how many transactions complete per unit of time. A system can have acceptable average response time and still have poor throughput under load, so both need explicit targets.

</details>

#### Q55. What is a performance baseline, and why is it useful?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A performance baseline is a recorded measurement of a known build and configuration under a defined workload. It gives future changes a comparison point for detecting regressions and validating tuning. A baseline is only meaningful when the workload, data, and environment are controlled.

</details>

#### Q56. How do performance, load, and stress testing differ?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Performance testing is the umbrella activity of evaluating speed, stability, and resource use under workload. Load testing applies expected or peak load, while stress testing exceeds capacity to find limits and failure behavior. The same tools may run all three, but they answer different release questions, and a release decision usually needs at least load and stress evidence.

</details>

### Other non-functional types

#### Q57. What is security testing, and what does QA cover at a basic level?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Security testing checks that the system protects data and resists misuse. At a basic QA level it covers authentication, authorization, input validation, session handling, sensitive data exposure, and common web risks. QA verifies security requirements and coordinates deeper penetration testing; it does not replace a security specialist.

</details>

#### Q58. What is usability testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Usability testing evaluates whether real users can complete tasks effectively, efficiently, and with satisfaction. It uses task-based sessions and observation rather than only checking whether a function works. Heuristic review is a complementary expert inspection, not a substitute for watching users. It finds friction and confusion that functional tests miss.

</details>

#### Q59. What is compatibility testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Compatibility testing verifies that the product works across supported browsers, operating systems, devices, resolutions, networks, and third-party integrations. A risk-based matrix of supported combinations is more practical than testing every permutation. Include backward and forward compatibility where versions and data formats matter.

</details>

#### Q60. What is accessibility testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Accessibility testing verifies that people with disabilities can use the product, covering keyboard navigation, screen-reader labels, contrast, focus order, captions, and relevant accessibility standards. It combines automated checks with manual testing using assistive technology. Accessibility problems carry usability, legal, and reputational risk, so they belong in release criteria.

</details>

#### Q61. What is localization and internationalization testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Internationalization is designing the product to support multiple locales; localization is adapting it to a specific locale's language, formats, and conventions. Testing covers translations, date and number formats, currency, text expansion, right-to-left layouts, and locale-specific data. It catches hard-coded assumptions that only appear outside the original market.

</details>

#### Q62. What is recovery testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Recovery testing verifies that the system can recover from crashes, network loss, dependency outages, or data corruption within an acceptable time and without losing data. It exercises backup and restore, failover, retries, and graceful degradation. Release QA cares because recovery behavior is difficult and expensive to repair after launch.

</details>

#### Q63. What is install, uninstall, and configuration testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

This testing verifies that the product installs, upgrades, configures, and uninstalls correctly across supported environments, including permissions, dependencies, and rollback. For a hosted service, the equivalent is deployment, configuration, and upgrade testing. It catches packaging and environment defects that functional tests never reach.

</details>

### Structural and execution styles

#### Q64. What are black-box, white-box, and gray-box testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Black-box testing checks behavior without knowledge of the internal implementation. White-box testing uses code and structure knowledge, as in unit testing and coverage analysis. Gray-box testing combines partial internal knowledge with external behavior, as in many API and integration tests.

</details>

#### Q65. What is the difference between static and dynamic testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Static testing examines artifacts without executing code: reviews, inspections, walkthroughs, and static analysis. Dynamic testing runs the product and observes its behavior. Static testing finds defects earlier and more cheaply, while dynamic testing proves runtime behavior; a release strategy needs both.

</details>

## Part 3 — Design Techniques and Methodologies (20 questions)

### Equivalence partitioning

#### Q66. What is equivalence partitioning?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Equivalence partitioning divides the input space into classes whose values should be handled the same way, then tests one representative from each valid and invalid class. It reduces the number of cases without giving up coverage of each behavior. The technique assumes that if one value in a class works, the others in that class will too.

</details>

#### Q67. How do you choose equivalence classes for a form field?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Derive classes from the field's rules: a valid range, values below and above it, the wrong data type, and blank or missing input. For an age field limited to 18 through 65, the classes are below 18, 18 to 65, above 65, non-numeric, and empty. Pick one representative from each class, and handle the exact edges separately with boundary value analysis.

</details>

### Boundary value analysis

#### Q68. What is boundary value analysis, and why are boundaries defect-prone?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Boundary value analysis tests values at the edges of each equivalence class, such as the minimum, minimum minus one, maximum, and maximum plus one. Boundaries are defect-prone because comparisons, loops, and limits often contain off-by-one errors. It catches defects that testing only the middle of a range would miss.

</details>

#### Q69. How do equivalence partitioning and boundary value analysis work together?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Equivalence partitioning chooses representative values for each class, while boundary value analysis adds the edges of those classes. For a field that accepts 1 through 100, partitioning might test 50 plus an invalid value, while boundary analysis tests 0, 1, 2, 99, 100, and 101. Together they cover both ordinary behavior and the limits with far fewer cases than exhaustive testing.

</details>

### Decision tables

#### Q70. What is decision table testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Decision table testing models combinations of conditions and the actions the system should take for each combination. Each rule or column describes one combination, including rules where conditions conflict or overlap. It is useful when the output depends on several inputs together rather than on one range at a time.

</details>

#### Q71. When is a decision table more useful than equivalence partitioning?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A decision table is more useful when the outcome depends on combinations of conditions, such as eligibility, pricing, discounts, or approval rules. Equivalence partitioning tests one condition at a time, so it can miss interactions between them. Decision tables make those interactions and any missing combinations visible.

</details>

### State transition testing

#### Q72. What is state transition testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

State transition testing models the states a system can be in, the events that move it between states, and any guards or actions attached to those transitions. Tests cover valid transitions and invalid ones, such as an event that should be rejected in the current state. It suits workflows, order lifecycles, sessions, and device modes.

</details>

#### Q73. What should a state transition test suite include beyond the happy path?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Cover every valid transition at least once, every invalid transition, guard conditions, entry and exit actions, and sequences that reach each state. Add reset and recovery paths and repeated or concurrent events where they are plausible. The goal is coverage of states and transitions, not just the main flow.

</details>

### Pairwise and combinatorial testing

#### Q74. What is pairwise testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Pairwise testing generates combinations so that every pair of parameter values appears at least once, instead of testing the full Cartesian product. Most interaction defects involve two factors, so pairwise often finds them with far fewer cases. It is useful for configuration matrices such as browsers, operating systems, devices, and feature options.

</details>

#### Q75. When would you not rely on pairwise testing alone?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Do not rely on pairwise alone when three-way or higher interactions are plausible, when specific combinations are high-risk or regulated, or when the order of events matters. Pairwise guarantees every pair but not every triple. Add targeted cases for critical combinations and domain rules; pairwise is a reduction technique, not a guarantee.

</details>

### Use case testing

#### Q76. What is use case testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Use case testing derives tests from end-to-end actor-and-goal scenarios, covering the main flow, alternate flows, and exception paths. It validates business workflows across multiple components rather than isolated functions. It complements requirement-level tests by exercising realistic user journeys.

</details>

### Error guessing

#### Q77. What is error guessing, and how do you make it repeatable?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Error guessing uses experience and intuition to target likely defects, such as empty inputs, special characters, interrupted flows, concurrency, and patterns from past failures. To make it repeatable, keep a checklist of historical defect patterns and convert recurring guesses into explicit test cases or exploratory charters. It works best alongside structured techniques, not instead of them.

</details>

### Exploratory testing

#### Q78. What is exploratory testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Exploratory testing combines learning, test design, and execution in the same session, guided by a charter rather than a fixed script. The tester explores the product, observes behavior, and adapts the next test based on what was learned. It finds issues that scripted tests miss, but it depends on skilled testers and needs time-boxing and notes.

</details>

#### Q79. How do you make exploratory testing auditable and useful for a release decision?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Define charters and target areas, time-box the sessions, and record what was covered and what was found. Review the notes with the team and convert critical discoveries into repeatable regression tests. Coverage is documented through charters and session notes rather than a count of prewritten cases.

</details>

### Risk-based testing

#### Q80. What is risk-based testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Risk-based testing prioritizes effort by the likelihood and impact of failure. High-risk areas get deeper techniques, more test data, and a larger regression share, while low-risk areas get lighter coverage. Risk can come from complexity, recent change, usage levels, defect history, and business criticality, and it should be reviewed as the release evolves.

</details>

#### Q81. How do you build a risk-based test strategy for a release?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Identify risks with product, development, and business stakeholders, then score likelihood and impact. Map each risk to test areas, techniques, and exit criteria, and allocate effort to the highest risks first. Document accepted residual risk and re-score when scope or changes alter the picture, so trade-offs are explicit when time is short.

</details>

### TDD

#### Q82. What is test-driven development, and how does it affect the test pyramid?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Test-driven development follows a red-green-refactor cycle: write a failing test, make it pass with the simplest code, then refactor while keeping tests green. It produces fine-grained unit tests and drives design toward testability. It complements rather than replaces integration and end-to-end testing, so the higher layers of the pyramid still matter.

</details>

### BDD

#### Q83. What is behavior-driven development, and what artifact does it produce?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Behavior-driven development is a collaboration approach that specifies behavior through concrete examples in business language, commonly in Given-When-Then form. Product, development, and QA agree on those examples before implementation, and they can become executable acceptance scenarios. It improves shared understanding and exposes ambiguous requirements, but the scenarios need good examples and maintenance.

</details>

### ATDD

#### Q84. What is acceptance-test-driven development, and how does it differ from BDD?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Acceptance-test-driven development starts by agreeing on acceptance tests or criteria before implementation, then builds the feature to satisfy them. BDD is a broader collaboration and example style, often expressed as Given-When-Then scenarios. Both shift testing left, and the distinction is blurry in practice; ATDD emphasizes the acceptance-test artifact and workflow.

</details>

### Test pyramid

#### Q85. What is the test pyramid, and how would you apply it in a release QA strategy?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The test pyramid calls for many fast unit tests, fewer integration or service tests, and a small number of end-to-end tests. It keeps feedback fast and maintenance low while still covering critical journeys. Apply it by pushing each check to the lowest level that can prove it, keeping a small critical end-to-end suite, and adding extra layers where risk demands them; the inverse anti-pattern is often called the ice-cream cone.

</details>

## Part 4 — Release QA in Practice (15 questions)

### Entry and exit criteria

#### Q86. What are entry criteria, and why does a release QA team need them?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Entry criteria are the preconditions for starting a test phase: approved requirements, a build available, a ready environment, prepared test data, and a passing smoke test. They stop the team from spending a regression cycle on a build that cannot be tested meaningfully. When entry criteria are not met, the team should start with known risk or wait, rather than quietly absorb the gap.

</details>

#### Q87. What are exit criteria, and how do you keep them from becoming a formality?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Exit criteria define when testing is complete: required coverage, pass rates, no open critical or high-severity defects, finished regression, and non-functional evidence. Keep them meaningful by agreeing them before testing starts, tying them to risk, and reviewing every exception explicitly. A green dashboard with untested high-risk areas is not a real exit.

</details>

### Release readiness and go/no-go

#### Q88. What does release readiness mean, and what evidence should support it?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Release readiness means the product, documentation, support, and operations are prepared for users. Supporting evidence includes the test summary, open defects and residual risk, regression and non-functional results, a deployment and rollback plan, monitoring, and known issues. It is a cross-functional judgment, not simply a statement that QA passed.

</details>

#### Q89. How would you run a go/no-go meeting?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Review the release scope, quality evidence, open defects, risks, dependencies, and rollback readiness against the agreed criteria. Each function states its readiness and concerns, and the decision owner records go, conditional go, or no-go with the rationale and owners. A conditional go must name the accepted risks and the follow-up actions.

</details>

### Regression scope selection

#### Q90. How do you decide what belongs in a release regression suite?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Include critical user journeys, high-risk and recently changed areas, integrations, non-functional thresholds, and modules with a history of defects. Automate the stable core and balance coverage against runtime and maintenance cost. Review the suite after each release and adjust it using incidents and escaped defects.

</details>

#### Q91. What do you do when the full regression suite cannot finish before the release date?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Re-prioritize by risk, run the highest-value subset, and add targeted tests for changed areas. Document what was skipped and the residual risk, and consider parallel environments, extra automation, or scope reduction. The release decision must see the gap; a green partial run must not be presented as full coverage.

</details>

### Defect severity, priority, and triage

#### Q92. What is the difference between defect severity and priority?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Severity describes the defect's impact on the system, such as a crash, data loss, or a broken workflow. Priority describes how urgently the business wants it fixed for this release. A cosmetic error on the homepage can be high priority but low severity, while a crash in a rarely used admin path can be high severity but low priority.

</details>

#### Q93. How does defect triage work during a release crunch?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Product, development, and QA review the open defects, confirm severity and priority, and decide to fix, defer, or accept each one with a documented rationale. Release-blocking, high-impact, and regression defects come first, while accepted issues need workarounds and named owners. Keep triage time-boxed and record the decisions so they remain visible after the release.

</details>

### Test summary report

#### Q94. What belongs in a test summary report?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A test summary report covers what was tested and what was not, the build and environment, results against exit criteria, defect counts by severity, coverage and pass rates, open risks, and a recommendation. It should be concise and decision-oriented, with links to the underlying evidence. It is not a dump of every test case.

</details>

### Release metrics

#### Q95. Which release metrics are genuinely useful, and which can mislead?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Useful metrics include escaped-defect rate, defect density and trend, pass rate by risk area, regression coverage, open critical defects, and test cycle time. Raw case counts, a bare "98% passed", or an automation percentage without reliability context can mislead. Pair every metric with its scope and trend so the release decision is not built on a single number.

</details>

### Requirements traceability

#### Q96. What is requirements traceability, and why does it matter at release?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Requirements traceability maps requirements to design, tests, defects, and evidence, usually through identifiers or a traceability matrix. It shows which requirements are covered, exposes untested ones, and supports impact analysis when something changes. At release, it is the evidence for what was verified and what was consciously left out.

</details>

### Hotfix verification

#### Q97. How should a hotfix be verified before and after deployment?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Before deployment, reproduce the original defect, verify the fix in a production-like environment, and run targeted regression around the affected areas. After deployment, run smoke and synthetic checks, monitor errors and latency, and confirm the user-facing behavior. A hotfix skips breadth, not discipline.

</details>

### Environment and configuration management

#### Q98. Why is environment and configuration management part of release QA?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Test results are only meaningful when the test environment matches the release configuration: versions, feature flags, data, and infrastructure. Drift produces "works in test, fails in production" and invalidates the evidence. QA should record environment versions, control configuration changes, and verify deployment parity.

</details>

### Release notes and known issues

#### Q99. What should release notes and known issues communicate to users and support?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Release notes should explain what changed, who is affected, how to use it, any required actions, and known limitations or workarounds with their impact. Clear notes set expectations and reduce support load. QA should review them for accuracy against tested behavior and the current open-defect list.

</details>

### Sign-off and risk acceptance

#### Q100. What does QA sign-off mean, and how should residual risk be handled?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

QA sign-off confirms that testing was performed against the agreed scope and criteria and reports the evidence and residual risk; it does not mean zero defects. Residual risks should be documented with impact, likelihood, workaround, owner, and decision. The release owner accepts them explicitly, and QA should not silently absorb business risk through its signature.

</details>
