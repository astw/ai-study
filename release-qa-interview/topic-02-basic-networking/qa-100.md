## Part 1 — Networking Fundamentals and Models (15 questions)

### Network types and components

#### Q1. What is a network, and what are nodes and links?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A network is two or more devices connected to exchange data. Each device is a node, and each connection is a link, which can be wired or wireless. Data is carried in packets rather than as one continuous stream.

</details>

#### Q2. What is the difference between a LAN, MAN, WAN, and PAN?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A PAN covers a person's devices, a LAN covers a building or campus, a MAN covers a city, and a WAN covers countries or the globe. The internet is the largest WAN. The size and ownership change, but the same packet forwarding applies.

</details>

#### Q3. What is the difference between the internet and an intranet?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The internet is the public network of networks; an intranet is a private network inside an organization. An intranet may use internet technologies such as HTTP and DNS but is not reachable from the public internet without a gateway. QA often tests the same application on both sides.

</details>

#### Q4. What is a packet, and why is data split into packets?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A packet is a small unit of data with a header and a payload. Splitting data into packets lets many devices share a link and lets routers forward each packet independently. It also means packets can arrive out of order or be lost, which TCP is designed to handle.

</details>

### Client-server vs peer-to-peer

#### Q5. What is client-server architecture?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

In client-server, clients request a service and a central server responds. The server is easier to control and back up, but it can become a bottleneck or a single point of failure. Web and email are typical examples.

</details>

#### Q6. What is peer-to-peer, and how does it differ from client-server?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

In peer-to-peer, devices share directly and each can act as both client and server; there is no dedicated central server. It can be cheaper and more resilient but harder to control and secure. File-sharing is the classic example.

</details>

### OSI and TCP/IP models

#### Q7. What are the seven OSI layers, and what does each do?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

OSI has seven layers: Physical (bits), Data Link (frames on one link), Network (IP routing), Transport (TCP or UDP delivery), Session (conversations), Presentation (formats and encryption), and Application (user protocols). Each layer serves the one above it. The layer names and jobs matter more than memorizing every protocol.

</details>

#### Q8. Which OSI layers map to which TCP/IP layers?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

TCP/IP's Application layer maps to OSI layers 5 through 7, Transport to layer 4, Internet to layer 3, and Link to layers 1 and 2. TCP/IP collapses the top three OSI layers because real protocols do not separate them cleanly. The mapping is a convenience, not a strict boundary.

</details>

#### Q9. Why do we use layered network models?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Layered models break a complex problem into independent jobs, so a change in one layer does not require rewriting the others. They also give testers a shared vocabulary for locating a fault: cable, IP, DNS, TCP, TLS, or application. The models are maps, not laws.

</details>

#### Q10. At which layer do HTTP, TCP, IP, and Ethernet operate?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

HTTP is an application-layer protocol, TCP is transport, IP is internet or network, and Ethernet is link and physical. TLS sits between transport and application in practice. Knowing the layer tells you which tool and team to involve.

</details>

#### Q11. What is the difference between a protocol and a layer?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A protocol is an agreed set of rules for communication, such as TCP, DNS, or HTTP. A layer is a level of responsibility in a model, and several protocols can live at one layer. For example, HTTP and DNS are both application-layer protocols.

</details>

### Encapsulation

#### Q12. What is encapsulation?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Encapsulation is adding a header at each layer as data moves down the stack. The receiver removes one header per layer as the data moves up. It is why a packet capture can show Ethernet, IP, TCP, and HTTP headers nested together.

</details>

#### Q13. What are the PDU names at each layer?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

At the application layer the unit is data; at transport it is a segment for TCP or a datagram for UDP; at the internet layer it is a packet; at the link layer it is a frame; at the physical layer it is bits. These names are common interview questions.

</details>

### Bandwidth, latency, and throughput

#### Q14. What is the difference between bandwidth and throughput?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Bandwidth is the maximum capacity of a link; throughput is the actual rate achieved. Throughput is limited by bandwidth but also by latency, loss, protocol overhead, and server speed. A high-bandwidth link can still have low throughput.

</details>

#### Q15. What are latency, jitter, and packet loss?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Latency is delay, measured in milliseconds; round-trip time is there and back. Jitter is variation in latency, which hurts voice and video. Packet loss is the percentage of packets that never arrive, causing retransmission on TCP or glitches on UDP.

</details>

## Part 2 — Topologies and Devices (15 questions)

### Topologies

#### Q16. What is the difference between physical and logical topology?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Physical topology is how devices and cables are arranged; logical topology is how traffic actually flows through subnets, VLANs, and routes. A site can be a physical star but a logical mix of VLANs. Troubleshooting needs both views.

</details>

#### Q17. What is a bus topology, and what is its main weakness?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A bus topology uses one shared backbone that every node taps into. It is cheap and simple, but a break in the backbone or heavy collisions can take down the whole segment. It is largely obsolete in LANs.

</details>

#### Q18. What is a star topology, and what is its single point of failure?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A star topology connects every device to a central switch or hub. Fault isolation is easy because each device has its own link, but the central device is a single point of failure. Most office LANs are physical stars.

</details>

#### Q19. What is a ring topology, and how does a dual ring help?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A ring connects nodes in a loop, so each node has a predictable neighbor. A single break disrupts a simple ring; a dual ring can survive one break by wrapping traffic the other way. Rings are more common in provider and legacy networks than in office LANs.

</details>

#### Q20. What is the difference between a full mesh and a partial mesh?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

In a full mesh, every node connects to every other node, giving maximum redundancy at high cost. A partial mesh connects some nodes to several others, balancing cost and resilience. WAN cores often use a partial mesh.

</details>

#### Q21. What is a tree topology?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A tree topology is a hierarchy of star networks, with access switches feeding distribution and core switches. It scales well across buildings and floors, but upper links and the root are single points of failure. Enterprise LANs commonly use it.

</details>

#### Q22. What is a hybrid topology, and why is it common?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A hybrid topology combines topologies, such as star access with a mesh core or a ring between sites. Real networks are usually hybrids because they mix equipment, budgets, and legacy designs. They are flexible but harder to document and troubleshoot.

</details>

### Devices

#### Q23. What is the difference between a hub and a switch?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A hub repeats incoming bits to every port, creating one collision domain. A switch learns MAC addresses and forwards frames only to the relevant port, giving each port its own collision domain. Switches are the modern default.

</details>

#### Q24. What does a router do?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A router forwards packets between different IP networks and is the default gateway for hosts. It breaks broadcast domains and often performs NAT. If the gateway is wrong, local traffic can work while remote traffic fails.

</details>

#### Q25. What does an access point do?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

An access point bridges Wi-Fi clients onto the wired LAN. It broadcasts an SSID, authenticates clients, and hands traffic to the switch. Coverage, roaming, and interference problems often look like application or internet failures.

</details>

#### Q26. What does a firewall do, and where does it sit?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A firewall filters traffic by rules and connection state, usually at layers 3 and 4 and sometimes at layer 7. It can block a port, allow only certain sources, or inspect application traffic. A blocked port often looks like a timeout rather than a refusal.

</details>

#### Q27. What is the difference between a proxy and a load balancer?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A proxy forwards requests on behalf of clients and can cache or filter them; a load balancer distributes requests across servers and checks their health. Both sit in the request path, but they solve different problems. A load balancer may also terminate TLS, so certificates and sessions can differ per node.

</details>

### Collision domains, broadcast domains, and VLANs

#### Q28. What is a collision domain, and how do switches change it?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A collision domain is the set of devices that can collide when transmitting at the same time. A hub extends one domain to all ports; a switch gives each port its own domain, and full-duplex ports do not collide. This is why replacing a hub with a switch improves performance.

</details>

#### Q29. What is a broadcast domain, and how do routers and VLANs affect it?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A broadcast domain is the set of devices that receive a broadcast frame. Switches forward broadcasts within a VLAN, so they extend the domain; routers do not forward broadcasts, so they break it. VLANs create separate broadcast domains on the same switches.

</details>

#### Q30. What is a VLAN, and why does it matter for testing?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A VLAN is a logical switch partition that creates a separate broadcast domain. Devices in different VLANs need routing and usually a firewall rule to communicate. When a test client cannot reach a server that is up, check whether they are in different VLANs or subnets.

</details>
