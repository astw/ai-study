Part one

Understand the machine

What an LLM actually does, and why fluent is not the same as true.

PredictionContextHallucinationVerification

Transition. We start with the mental model because every practical habit follows from it.

If the audience remembers one idea from this section, make it this: the model produces plausible text, not verified facts.

The mechanism

It predicts the next token. That is the whole trick.

At sufficient scale, next-token prediction produces something that looks like reasoning. The machinery is still a guess about what comes next.

It is completing a pattern. It is not consulting a source.

  • Trained on text, not on a database of verified facts.
  • Each token is sampled from a probability distribution.
  • The same prompt can produce a different answer.
Useful mental model: a very well-read improv partner with no fact-checker.

Handbook: Part 1.1–1.3 covers tokens, training, inference, and why the same prompt varies.

The percentages are illustrative. The important part is the shape: a distribution over candidates, then a sample.

Do not overclaim. Modern models can reason over text in useful ways. The point is that the output is generated, not retrieved from a truth store.

Three consequences

Why it sounds right and is sometimes wrong.

These are properties of the technology, not bugs you can patch with a better prompt.

Knowledge cutoff

It does not know what happened after training.

New papers, changed policies, and this morning's news may be missing or stale.

Fix: give it current sources, or use a tool that can search.

Hallucination

It fills gaps with plausible text.

A fake citation looks exactly like a real one until you check it.

Fix: require quotes, locations, and a source you can open.

Non-determinism

The same prompt can give a different answer.

Sampling makes the output vary. That is useful for brainstorming and risky for facts.

Fix: constrain the format, rerun, and compare.

Ask the room: which of these have you already seen? Most people have a hallucinated citation or a confident wrong answer story.

Handbook: Part 1.5–1.6. The practical rule is to ground the model in sources and verify anything that matters.

Working memory

The context window is a desk, not a filing cabinet.

The model sees only what is on the desk right now. Everything else might as well not exist.

  • Relevance beats volume. Irrelevant context dilutes attention.
  • One task per session. Switch tasks, start clean.
  • Use the right command. /clear when you switch; /compact when a long session must continue.
In Claude Code, the desk is assembled fresh each turn from the conversation, your files, and tool results.

Handbook: Part 1.6 and Part 3.5. The desk metaphor is the one to keep.

If the model seems to forget something, ask what is actually in context. A file on disk is not automatically on the desk.

Chat vs. agent

A chatbot answers. An agent acts.

The model is the brain. The tools are the hands. You are the judgment.

Chatbot: you paste text, it replies, you copy the answer.
Agent: it reads files, runs commands, edits files, checks results, and repeats.

Claude Code works on your project folder. That folder is the shared workspace between you and the agent.

Handbook: Part 1.4 and Part 3.2. A chatbot produces text; an agent changes state in a workspace.

This is why permissions matter. Once the model can run tools, a wrong action can change a file or run a command.

Fit

Use it where it is strong.

The same tool can be excellent and useless in the same afternoon. The task decides.

Strong
  • Drafting, rewriting, and changing tone.
  • Summarizing and explaining material you provide.
  • Transforming text into tables, cards, or code.
  • Brainstorming options and finding patterns.
  • Writing and debugging code with tests.
Weak without help
  • Facts after its training cutoff.
  • Precise arithmetic without a tool.
  • Citations recalled from memory.
  • Knowing your intent without context.
  • High-stakes decisions that need accountability.
  • Anything involving secrets or private data.
Ground it. Check it. Or do not ship it. That sentence covers most of the safety advice in this deck.

Handbook: Part 1.7. Give concrete examples from study, writing, and coding.

The list is not permanent. Models improve. The habit is to ask: what would count as evidence that this answer is right?

Hallucination

Confidence is a style. It is not evidence.

A fabricated citation is not a rare glitch. It is the same next-token machinery doing exactly what it was trained to do.

An illustrative citation

“Example et al. (2023) found that sleep improves recall by 40%.”

Journal of Illustrative Results, 12(3), 44–58.

No such paper

The model is not lying. It is completing a plausible pattern.

Three verification habits

  • Give it the source. Ask it to answer only from the text you provide.
  • Require quotes and locations. A page number or section heading makes the claim checkable.
  • Cross-check names, numbers, and citations. Open the link or search the DOI yourself.
If you cannot verify it, describe it as a draft, not a fact.

Handbook: Part 1.5 and Part 2.6. The citation is clearly labeled as illustrative; do not present it as real.

Ask the audience for a time they trusted a confident answer. Then ask what checking would have cost. Usually it is one search.

The rules

Three rules for every session.

They apply to a study session, a coding task, and a group project.

01

Verify before you rely.

Every fact, quote, and citation gets a second look. The model's confidence tells you nothing about its accuracy.

02

Protect private data.

No passwords, medical records, or other people's work without permission. Check your school's policy.

03

Keep your own thinking.

Use AI to sharpen a draft, not to replace your judgment. You own the claim and the error.

AI can write the draft. You own the claim. That is the line to remember when a deadline is close.

Handbook: Part 1.9 and Part 7.6. Academic integrity policies vary by institution and by assignment.

If you are unsure, ask the instructor before you use AI on graded work. Keep your drafts so you can show your process.

Ask better

Prompting is briefing. The goal is to leave less room for guessing.

The six-part briefTechniquesPatternsInjection

Transition. The model is a guesser. A prompt is how you narrow the space of plausible answers.

This section is practical. The audience should leave with a template and a few habits, not a theory of prompt engineering.

The brief

A good prompt is a brief.

Six parts. When an answer disappoints, one of them is usually missing.

Role: Who should it act as?
Task: What exactly should it do?
Context: What does it need to know?
Input: What material should it use?
Rules: What must it avoid?
Output: What shape should the answer take?
A complete brief 
Role: PSY 101 tutor.
Task: Make 10 flashcards.
Context: Exam covers lectures 1–3.
Input: @notes/lecture-03.md
Rules: One idea per card; no yes/no;
       use only the notes.
Output: cards.md format.

The labels are a checklist, not a ritual. Short prompts can still be complete.

Handbook: Part 2.2. The six parts are role, task, context, input, rules, and output.

Do not add all six every time. Add the ones that remove ambiguity for this task. “Role” is useful for vocabulary and standards, not magic.

Before / after

Same task. Different brief.

The weak prompt is not wrong. It is under-specified, so the model fills the gaps for you.

BeforeVague
Summarize this article.

Missing: audience, length, structure, and a rule for claims outside the article.

AfterUsable
You are a research assistant.
Summarize this article for a student
who has 10 minutes. Use 5 bullets,
one per section. Quote the thesis.
If a claim is not in the article, say so.

What changed: role, audience, shape, grounding.

Role→Audience→Shape→Grounding

Handbook: Part 2.2–2.3. The “after” prompt is longer, but most of the length is information the model would otherwise invent.

Ask the room to rewrite “make me flashcards for psych” using the six parts. That is the exercise in the handbook.

Techniques 1–3

Three moves that fix most prompts.

Specificity is not about sounding formal. It is about reducing the number of plausible answers.

01

Specify the output.

Length, format, audience, constraints. If the shape matters, say it.

Try: “Answer in five bullets for a first-year student.”

02

Show an example.

One good example teaches style faster than a paragraph of rules.

Try: “Here are two cards I like. Match their style.”

03

Plan first.

Ask for the steps and the files before any edits. You can catch a wrong direction early.

Try: “List a plan. Do not edit yet.”

Plan-first is also how plan mode works in Claude Code. You will see it again in Part 3.

Handbook: Part 2.3. Few-shot examples, output schemas, and plan-first prompting.

Ask for a plan is often the single highest-leverage line for a beginner. It turns a surprise into a conversation.

Techniques 4–6

Then make it a conversation.

The first answer is a draft. The useful work happens in the next turn.

04

Let it ask.

“What do you need from me before you start?” surfaces assumptions early.

05

Require evidence.

“Quote the source. Mark uncertainty.” A confident answer without a source is a lead, not a fact.

06

Iterate.

“Critique this against the brief, then revise.” You get a second pass without starting over.

Draft→Critique→Revise→Check

Handbook: Part 2.4–2.5. The critique-and-revise loop is the most reusable pattern in the deck.

Show a live example if possible: ask for a draft, then ask “what is weak, and what would you change?” The second answer is usually better.

Prompt library

Six prompts worth stealing.

Adapt the wording to your course and your voice. Keep the ones that save you time.

Explain

Build intuition before memorization.

“Explain X to a first-year student with one analogy and one worked example.”

Study

Turn notes into retrieval practice.

“Turn these notes into 10 retrieval-practice questions. Keep the answer key separate.”

Critique

Get feedback against a real rubric.

“Review this draft against the rubric. Quote the line and name the fix.”

Debug

Find the cheapest test first.

“Here is the error and the file. List three hypotheses, then test the cheapest one.”

Research

Keep the model inside your sources.

“Summarize only from these sources. Cite page numbers and mark gaps.”

Plan

Make the work visible before you start.

“Ask me clarifying questions, then propose a plan with risks.”

Handbook: Part 2.5 and Appendix B have a larger prompt library.

Encourage students to keep a personal file of prompts that worked. A prompt library is a study skill, not a collection of magic words.

Failure modes

What not to do.

Most bad AI sessions are not model failures. They are missing context, missing verification, or missing boundaries.

  • “Do my assignment.” No context, no learning, and probably a policy violation.
  • “Make it better.” No target. Better for whom, and by what measure?
  • “It sounds confident.” Confidence is not evidence.
  • “Paste everything.” Context dilution makes the important part harder to find.
  • “Trust the citation.” Open it. Search it. Check it.
Prompt injection: anything the model reads can contain instructions. A PDF, a web page, or a GitHub issue can say “ignore previous instructions.” Treat that text as data, not as a command from you.

Keep a human in the loop for anything that matters: sending, deleting, paying, submitting, or publishing.

Handbook: Part 2.7 and Part 8.5. Prompt injection is a security issue, not just a prompting issue.

The practical rule: separate instructions you wrote from content the model fetched. If a document tells the agent to do something, that is a red flag.

What it is

One folder. One agent. A loop.

Claude Code works on your project folder. It reads, edits, runs commands, and checks results.

The model

Reads and reasons.

It plans the next step, chooses a tool, and interprets what comes back.

The tools

Files, shell, search, web.

Reading, writing, editing, running tests, searching a codebase, and looking things up.

You

Goal, permissions, review.

You set the task, approve risky actions, and decide whether the result is good.

The model is the brain. The tools are the hands. You are the judgment.

Handbook: Part 3.2–3.4. Claude Code is available in the terminal, desktop app, IDE extensions, and on the web.

It is not autocomplete. The unit of work is a task in a folder, and the output is a changed workspace plus a conversation.

The interface

Four keys do most of the work.

You type in plain language. These four keys give the agent context and power.

terminal · ~/flashcards
> @notes/lecture-03.md make 10 cards Reading notes/lecture-03.md... > !python flashcards.py --check OK: 24 cards > /context 34% of context used
@ reference a file. Puts the file's contents into the conversation.
! run a shell command. The output comes back into context.
/ run a command. Examples: /status, /clear, /plan.
Shift+Tab change mode. Manual, accept-edits, plan, auto, or bypass.

Context

Keep the desk clean.

Long sessions do not just cost more. They make it harder for the model to find the part that matters.

/context shows what is on the desk. Look before you add more.
/compact summarizes and continues. Use it when a long session must keep going.
/clear starts fresh. Use it when you switch tasks or the conversation gets tangled.

Stable project rules belong in CLAUDE.md, not in every prompt.

Handbook: Part 3.5. One task per session is the simplest rule that prevents most context problems.

If the agent starts repeating itself or ignoring instructions, clear and restate the task. Do not keep adding corrections to a polluted context.

Project memory

Write the rules once.

A short project file saves you from repeating the same instructions every session.

# Flashcards project

## Commands
- Test: python -m unittest discover
- Check: python flashcards.py --check

## Conventions
- Python 3.10+, standard library only.
- cards.md is the source of truth.
- Never edit files in notes/.

## Done
Tests pass; quiz.html opens in a browser.

Where it lives

  • Project: ./CLAUDE.md, committed to the repo.
  • You: ~/.claude/CLAUDE.md, for personal preferences.
  • Local: CLAUDE.local.md, gitignored.

Draft it with /init. Keep it under 200 lines. It is context, not enforcement.

If a rule must never be broken, use permissions, not prose.

Handbook: Part 3.6. The file is loaded as context, so it can be ignored or overridden by other context. It is not a security boundary.

Good CLAUDE.md content: commands, conventions, definitions of done, and a few things never to touch. Bad content: a novel about the project.

The loop

A repeatable session.

This is the habit that makes agents reliable. It works for code, writing, and research.

01

Plan. Read the folder. Propose a plan. Do not edit yet.

02

Slice. Implement one behavior and its test.

03

Verify. Run the tests. Read the diff.

04

Review. Ask a subagent, or a fresh session, to critique.

05

Save. Commit. Update CLAUDE.md if the rules changed.

Small step → verify → save. Repeat. The loop matters more than the model you pick.

Handbook: Part 4.1 and Part 6.5. Plan mode is the first step; /rewind is the safety net for a wrong turn.

If a session goes badly, do not keep pushing. Rewind or clear, restate the task, and start from the last good state.

Skills

A skill is a recipe the model can load.

Use a skill when you repeat the same instructions more than twice.

.claude/skills/make-cards/SKILL.md 
---
name: make-cards
description: Convert a lecture note into
  cards in this project's format.
---
# Make cards
1. Read $ARGUMENTS.
2. Find 8–12 testable concepts.
3. Append cards; no duplicates.
4. Run python flashcards.py --check.

The description is the trigger. Write it like a search result, not a title.

Handbook: Part 4.3 and Part 6.6. Skills live in .claude/skills/<name>/SKILL.md. Keep the body under 500 lines.

Frontmatter can also set allowed-tools, argument-hint, and other fields. The description is the part the model uses to decide whether to load the skill.

Subagents

A subagent is a fresh pair of eyes.

It gets its own context and tools. Only the summary comes back to you.

Use one when

  • Research spans many files.
  • You want an independent review.
  • A task would flood your main context.

Example

card-reviewer checks every card against the notes, then returns a table of severity, card, problem, and suggested fix. It does not edit files.

Handbook: Part 4.4 and Part 6.8. Subagents live in .claude/agents/<name>.md. The description drives automatic delegation.

You can also ask explicitly: “use the card-reviewer subagent.” Built-ins include Explore, Plan, and general-purpose. The main benefit is context isolation, not magic intelligence.

RAG

Give the model the right pages.

Retrieval-augmented generation is a pipeline. The model is only one stage.

QuestionWhat the user asked
→
RetrieveSearch the sources
→
RerankKeep the best chunks
→
AugmentPut them in the prompt
→
GenerateAnswer from them
→
CiteShow where it came from

Failure modes

  • Bad chunking splits an idea in half.
  • Vocabulary mismatch hides the right passage.
  • The index is stale or incomplete.
  • The model cites a source it did not use.

The rule

If the right passage is not retrieved, the model cannot use it.

Better retrieval beats a longer prompt. Search quality sets the ceiling.

Handbook: Part 5.3. RAG is an architecture: collect, chunk, embed, index, retrieve, rerank, augment, generate.

The failure modes are worth more than the acronym. Most RAG demos fail at retrieval or chunking, not at generation.

RAG in practice

You may not need a vector database.

Claude Code already does a version of retrieval with files, search, and subagents.

@notes/lecture-03.md puts one file on the desk.
/add-dir ../research adds another folder to the workspace.
CLAUDE.md keeps stable project knowledge close to every turn.
Grep and Glob find exact strings and files without loading everything.
An Explore subagent searches many files and returns only the conclusion.
Build a real RAG when the corpus is large, shared, changing, or needs citations at scale. For one course folder, start with files and search.

Handbook: Part 5.3 and Part 6.9. The practical progression is @ references, then /add-dir, then search tools, then a real retrieval system.

This is the most important slide for students: do not build infrastructure you do not need. A clean folder and a good CLAUDE.md are often enough.

Academic integrity

Use AI without outsourcing your brain.

The rules vary by school, course, and assignment. Your syllabus overrides this slide.

Usually fine

  • Brainstorming ideas
  • Explaining a concept
  • Debugging your code
  • Editing for clarity
  • Generating practice questions

Ask first

  • Group work
  • Graded drafts
  • Lab reports
  • Code assignments
  • Anything covered by a policy

Never

  • Submit AI text as your own where prohibited
  • Fabricate a citation or source
  • Share confidential data
  • Let AI make a decision you must defend
If disclosure is required, try: “I used Claude to brainstorm and edit. All claims and errors are my own.”

Handbook: Part 7.6. Policies differ. Some courses allow editing but not drafting; some allow both with disclosure.

Keep your drafts and notes. If you are asked how you used AI, you should be able to show your process.

The first month

Twenty minutes a day.

A small daily practice beats one long session you never repeat.

The goal is not to use every tool. The goal is to build one reliable loop and then add a tool when a real task demands it.

The takeaway

A loop beats memorizing commands.

Models will change. These three habits will survive the next version.

01

Brief well.

Give the model context, rules, and a target output. Then ask for a plan.

02

Verify.

Use sources, diffs, and tests. Confidence is not evidence.

03

Stay in charge.

Approve risky actions. Keep your own judgment. The claim is yours.
